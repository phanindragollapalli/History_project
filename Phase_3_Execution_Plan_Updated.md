# Phase 3 Execution Plan: Historical Interpretation and Final Report

## Project

**Working title:** *Changing Patterns of Occupation, Ecological Niches, and Socio-Economic Interdependence in Chamba District (1881–1961)*

**Research question:**  
How did changing patterns of occupation and economic interdependence shape caste and religious relations in Brahmaur, Chitrari and Maingal of Chamba district from the late nineteenth century to 1961?

**Team:** Bala Subramanian, Vagdevi Reddy, Phanindra

**Final deliverable:** A 3,000–4,000-word historical essay that develops an evidence-based argument using Marxist and/or Annales approaches and computational analysis.

---

## 1. What Phase 3 must accomplish

Phase 1 established the historical problem, research question, sources and proposed methods. Phase 2 documented source processing, data extraction, computational analysis and preliminary findings. Phase 3 must turn that material into a defensible historical interpretation.

The final report must do all of the following:

1. State a clear historical thesis that directly answers the research question.
2. Explain how material conditions—land, labour, livestock, tools, markets and customary payments—shaped social relations.
3. Explain how geography, altitude, climate, seasonal mobility and ecological differences shaped economic possibilities over the long term.
4. Compare Brahmaur, Chitrari and Maingal rather than treating them as interchangeable examples.
5. Use the 1881, 1910/1911–19, 1931 and 1961 sources carefully, distinguishing what each source can establish.
6. Integrate computational results into the historical reasoning. A chart or frequency count must be interpreted, not merely displayed.
7. Critically assess colonial classifications, census categories, OCR errors, source silences and differences in scale.
8. Distinguish documented evidence from inference and acknowledge alternative explanations.
9. End with a conclusion that answers the research question and explains the contribution and limits of the project.

**Do not make Phase 3 simply a longer version of Phase 2.** Phase 2 asks what patterns can be extracted. Phase 3 asks why those patterns matter historically, how they relate to one another, and how far the evidence supports an explanation.

## 2. Recommended central argument

Use the following as a working thesis, to be revised after checking the primary sources:

> Between the late nineteenth century and 1961, caste and religious relations in the upper Ravi region of Chamba were shaped not only by inherited status categories but also by the material organisation of work and exchange. The ecological differences between alpine Brahmaur, terraced Chitrari and forested Maingal supported distinct livelihood systems. These systems created specific forms of dependence between pastoralists, cultivators, artisans and service groups. Customary obligations did not simply disappear with market exchange: wage payments, crop shares, grain perquisites, ritual exchanges and commercial dairy transactions co-existed. The evidence therefore suggests that social hierarchy and interdependence were reproduced and negotiated through locally specific economies, within ecological constraints and changing systems of classification.

This is a **working interpretation**, not a conclusion to assume in advance. Retain, qualify or reject each part according to the source evidence.

### Possible sub-arguments

- **Ecology and livelihood:** Altitude and climate influenced which livestock and productive activities were viable in each village.
- **Labour and hierarchy:** Landholding, control over productive resources and access to labour structured relations between dominant groups and occupational specialists.
- **Custom and markets:** Cash wages and commercial sales co-existed with customary grain, food, wool and ritual payments.
- **Religious relations and economic complementarity:** Some Hindu–Muslim interactions were organised through exchange between different livelihood systems; this does not, by itself, prove an absence of conflict.
- **Historical classification:** Colonial censuses and ethnographies represented communities through categories that changed over time and should not be treated as transparent descriptions of social identity.

## 4. Develop the theoretical framework

The course announcement asks for a historical argument drawing on Annales and/or Marxist methods. The project already proposes both. Use them as analytical methods, not as labels added to the introduction.

### 4.1 Marxist / historical materialist analysis

Ask how the organisation of production and control over resources shaped social relations.

Investigate:

- Who owned or controlled land, livestock, tools and productive resources?
- Who supplied labour, and under what conditions?
- Which groups depended on the labour or skills of other groups?
- What forms of compensation existed: cash wages, crop shares, grain, food, wool, meat or other customary perquisites?
- Who controlled access to markets and the circulation of commodities?
- Did the evidence indicate bargaining, mobility, resistance or attempts to renegotiate status?
- How did caste or religious identity intersect with economic position?

**Apply this to the three villages:**

- **Brahmaur:** Examine relations between flock owners and hired shepherds, Sipi wool workers/weavers and ritual specialists. Analyse the combination of contractual wages and non-cash perquisites. Ask who controlled flocks and who performed specialised labour.
- **Chitrari:** Examine landholding, temple-linked endowments, plough cultivation and attached Hali labour. Consider the reported Arya–Hali identification as a possible form of status contestation, while avoiding claims about its scale or success beyond the evidence.
- **Maingal:** Examine Gujjar buffalo pastoralism, ghee marketing, cultivators' access to manure or draft animals, and Lohar tool repair in exchange for khalian grain. Analyse how market transactions and customary obligations operated together.

Do not assume that every customary exchange was exploitative, that every cash payment represented freedom, or that every occupational group had a uniform experience. Use the evidence to establish the terms and asymmetries of each relationship.

### 4.2 Annales: longue durée and environmental history

Use the Annales approach to examine structures that changed slowly and shaped everyday possibilities.

Investigate:

- Altitude, relief, climate and seasonal snow conditions.
- Differences between alpine pasture, terraced agricultural land and forested slopes.
- Seasonal migration and the timing of pastoral and agricultural work.
- Livestock suitability and the limits imposed by animal biology.
- The relationship between settlement patterns, water, pasture, fields and transport.
- Material technologies such as the plough, iron share, shears, loom, kotha and machan.
- Everyday and seasonal rhythms that may be less visible in administrative records.

**Apply this comparatively:**

- **Brahmaur:** Alpine conditions, sheep/goat pastoralism, transhumance, wool processing and the Chaurasi ritual economy.
- **Chitrari:** Terraced agriculture, plough animals, temple-linked landholding and seasonal dairy exchange with mobile pastoralists.
- **Maingal:** Forest settlement, buffalo pastoralism, ghee production and exchange with cultivators and the town market.

Avoid environmental determinism. Geography shaped opportunities and constraints, but people made choices within those constraints. The reported buffalo episode in Brahmaur may support a specific ecological explanation, but one recorded trial cannot establish a universal biological law.

### 4.3 Combine the approaches

A useful analytical sequence is:

**Ecological conditions → available productive strategies → organisation of labour and resources → forms of exchange and dependence → social relations and status negotiation.**

This is a framework for testing evidence, not a mechanical chain. Political institutions, religious practices, migration, markets and historical contingencies may also matter.

## 5. Turn the computational work into historical evidence

The computational methods should support the argument. Include only methods that were actually implemented and whose outputs can be reproduced or verified.

### 5.1 Quantitative comparison

Use the verified dataset and the additional comparative occupational/economic structure analysis to produce a compact comparative table covering:

- Population and household composition
- Community/caste counts and percentages
- Livestock by species
- Occupational categories
- Recorded forms of payment or exchange
- Source year and geographical scale

For each measure, state the denominator and source. Mark missing or non-comparable values as such rather than filling them with zero.

### 5.2 Normalized word frequencies

The Phase 2 report calculates mentions per 1,000 searchable words:

`Normalized frequency = (keyword hits / total searchable words) × 1,000`

Use this as a measure of **textual prominence**, not as a direct measure of population size, economic importance or social power.

Tasks:

1. Recompute the values from the final cleaned text.
2. Document the keyword dictionary and variant spellings.
3. State whether the denominator is the full monograph or a selected section.
4. Check that OCR quality and text coverage do not differ in ways that distort comparison.
5. Use a small number of interpretable comparisons, such as sheep, buffalo, Gujjar, Sipi, Hali and Lohar.
6. Compare frequency patterns with the village tables and cited passages.
7. Explain mismatches. A word can be frequently discussed without the corresponding group being numerically dominant.

### 5.3 Keyword-in-Context (KWIC) and close reading

For each central term—such as *pohal*, *Sipi*, *Hali*, *Arya*, *Lohar*, *khalian*, *Gujjar*, *buffalo*, *ghee* and *transhumance*—select a small set of contextual passages.

For each passage:

- Preserve the original wording.
- Record page and source.
- Identify who is speaking: census enumerator, village survey author, community member or quoted oral voice.
- Record the action or relationship described.
- Interpret what the passage shows and what it does not show.

Use KWIC to locate evidence, then read the complete passage and surrounding pages. A keyword window alone is not enough to establish historical meaning.

### 5.4 Network analysis

Use the verified 12-relationship register and the additional network analysis to show who exchanged what with whom. Before presenting a network:

1. Define the nodes: communities, occupational groups, institutions, households or markets.
2. Define the edges: a documented exchange relationship.
3. Record the exchange medium: labour, grain, wool, meat, wages, dairy products, manure, tools or dues.
4. Attach a source citation to every edge.
5. Separate 1961 village-level relationships from regional or earlier historical relationships.
6. Do not treat the number of recorded edges as a measure of the actual intensity of social relations; the register reflects what the sources document.

Interpret the network historically. For example, ask whether the pattern shows that specialised groups were economically indispensable despite their lower or contested status, and whether different payment systems reveal a mixed economy.

### 5.5 Oral and material evidence

Use the folk verses and material culture descriptions as evidence about experience, work and social meaning, not as decoration.

Potential examples already identified in Phase 2:

- Chitrari women's song about a husband's seasonal absence and the hardship of ascent.
- Brahmaur's *Shiv Ainchali* and the reported multi-caste ritual assembly.
- *Bhohku Git* and the shepherd's death on a frozen pass.
- The wooden plough and iron share; *katar* shears; *rachh* loom; *kilta* basket; Gujjar *kotha* and *machan*.

For each item, explain what it reveals about labour, technology, mobility, gender, risk or ritual. Check the transcription and page reference. Do not treat a song as a literal census record or assume that a single verse represents every member of a community.

## 5A. Four additional computational analyses to strengthen Phase 3

The existing Phase 2 analysis is sufficient to begin historical writing. The following four analyses are recommended as targeted additions, not as a new computational project. They should be completed only to the extent that the underlying source data can support them. Their purpose is to strengthen comparison, test the reliability of existing interpretations and make the final historical argument more transparent.

### Analysis 1: Comparative occupational and economic structure

**Priority: Essential / highest priority**

**Historical question:** How did the economic organisation of Brahmaur, Chitrari and Maingal differ, and what forms of labour and exchange were associated with each local economy?

#### Data to assemble

Create one harmonised village comparison dataset. Suggested fields:

- Village
- Source and source page/table
- Survey year
- Total population and total households (where available)
- Community/caste/religious group
- Number of persons and/or households in that group
- Occupation or livelihood
- Livestock species and count
- Productive resource (land, flock, buffalo, plough animals, tools, forest/pasture)
- Labour relationship
- Payment or exchange form
- Evidence type and verification status

Keep persons, households, animals and textual mentions in separate fields. Do not combine them into one numerical measure.

#### Computation

1. Calculate community shares only where a reliable village population denominator is available.
2. Calculate livestock composition by species within each village, where the source provides a complete or clearly bounded enumeration.
3. Tabulate the major occupational and livelihood categories.
4. Classify documented exchanges as monetary, non-monetary or mixed.
5. Produce a side-by-side comparative table for the three villages.
6. Where the source data are incomplete or categories differ, mark the value as `NA` and explain why. Do not convert missing information into zero.

#### Outputs

- One harmonised CSV/Excel dataset.
- One comparative table for the report.
- A livestock composition chart, if the underlying counts are comparable.
- A concise written interpretation of the economic differences.

#### Historical interpretation

Use the comparison to examine whether the different ecological settings were associated with distinct productive systems and occupational specialisations. Then ask how those systems shaped dependence between landholders, pastoralists, artisans and service groups.

**Caution:** A larger livestock count does not automatically mean greater wealth or economic power. A community percentage does not by itself establish control over land or resources. Interpret each measure in relation to its source and denominator.

### Analysis 2: Economic interdependence network analysis

**Priority: Essential / highest priority**

**Historical question:** Which communities and occupational groups were connected through documented exchanges, and what kinds of resources or services linked them?

Phase 2 already identifies 12 relationships. Do not simply add more edges to make the network look larger. First validate the existing register and clarify which relationships belong to the 1961 village-level network and which are regional or from earlier sources.

#### Data to assemble

Create an edge list with these fields:

- Relationship ID
- Period/year
- Village or region
- Actor A
- Actor B
- Actor type for each actor (community, occupation, institution, market, state)
- Exchange medium
- Exchange category: monetary / non-monetary / mixed / dues or obligation
- Direction, if the source clearly specifies it
- Source title
- Page/paragraph/table
- Exact supporting passage or concise evidence note
- Verification status

Create a separate node list if useful, with each actor's standardised name, original source term, actor type and village/region.

#### Computation

1. Represent each documented exchange as an edge between two actors.
2. Use a bipartite representation only if the two node sets are genuinely distinct—for example, communities/occupational groups on one side and exchange media or activities on the other. If both sides contain social actors, use an actor-to-actor network instead.
3. Calculate simple descriptive measures only where meaningful: number of documented links per actor, number of exchange types, and distribution of monetary versus non-monetary links.
4. Compare the network structure across Brahmaur, Chitrari and Maingal.
5. Keep the regional 1881 dual-tenure relationship and any other cross-period examples visually or analytically distinct from the 1961 village-level network.
6. Attach a source reference to every edge and retain the evidence register behind the visualisation.

#### Outputs

- Verified edge-list dataset.
- Network diagram or exchange matrix.
- Small summary table of relationship types by village.
- Short interpretation of which forms of labour and exchange connected the groups.

#### Historical interpretation

Use the network to explore whether groups with different social status were economically interdependent, and whether that interdependence operated through wages, customary obligations, ritual exchange, market transactions or ecological transfers.

**Caution:** The network represents relationships documented in the surviving sources, not the complete social network of historical Chamba. Degree or link counts are counts of recorded evidence, not direct measures of power, wealth or actual interaction intensity.

### Analysis 3: Historical classification crosswalk, 1881–1961

**Priority: High**

**Historical question:** How did the sources classify communities, occupations and economic roles across the period, and how much of the apparent change reflects social transformation versus changing administrative categories?

This analysis is particularly important because the project compares colonial censuses, an ethnographic glossary and post-independence village monographs. These sources do not use identical categories.

#### Data to assemble

Create a crosswalk with these fields:

- Source and year
- Original term exactly as printed
- Page/table/paragraph
- Source context and author/agency
- Category type (caste, tribe/community, religion, occupation, livelihood, earner/dependency status)
- Normalised analytical category
- Proposed relationship to terms in other sources
- Comparability level: direct / partial / not comparable
- Notes on bias, ambiguity or uncertainty

Include the exact Rose edition and publication date after verifying the volume actually used. Phase 1 and Phase 2 currently describe the date differently, so resolve this before final submission.

#### Computation

1. Extract the relevant terms from the 1881 Census, the verified Rose volume, the 1931 Census and the 1961 village surveys.
2. Standardise spelling variants for analysis while preserving the original wording.
3. Group terms into broad analytical families only where the source context justifies it—for example, pastoral work, agricultural labour, artisan work or service.
4. Create a source-by-category matrix showing where a category is recorded, omitted, renamed or reclassified.
5. Calculate term frequencies only where corpus coverage and search rules are sufficiently comparable.
6. Mark categories as not comparable when a source's classification logic differs substantially from another source's.

#### Outputs

- Historical classification crosswalk.
- Source-by-category matrix or timeline.
- Short account of persistent, changing and non-comparable terms.

#### Historical interpretation

Use the crosswalk to distinguish at least three possibilities:

- A change in the actual organisation of work or social relations.
- A change in the administrative or ethnographic vocabulary used to describe them.
- A change in what the source chose to enumerate or make visible.

The 1931 emphasis on principal earners and working/non-working dependents should be analysed as a change in the census's classificatory framework, not automatically as proof that caste or hereditary occupational relations had disappeared.

**Caution:** A term disappearing from a source does not prove that the group or occupation disappeared from society. A new label does not necessarily mean a new social group emerged.

### Analysis 4: Qualitative thematic coding of the 20 passages

**Priority: Useful and recommended**

**Historical question:** What recurring themes emerge from the qualitative passages, and how do they illuminate labour, exchange, mobility, ritual, gender and everyday experience in the three villages?

The 20 passages already assembled in Phase 2 are a manageable starting corpus. Do not assume they are a representative sample of all village experience.

#### Suggested coding framework

Use a multi-label coding scheme. A passage may receive more than one theme.

| Code | Theme | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| LAB | Labour and occupational hierarchy | Who performs work; under what conditions; who controls resources |
| DEP | Economic dependence | Reliance on another group for labour, goods, services or access |
| EXC | Customary and monetary exchange | Wages, crop shares, grain, wool, food, meat, cash or market sales |
| ECO | Ecology and environment | Altitude, climate, pasture, forest, water, animal suitability |
| MOB | Mobility and seasonality | Transhumance, seasonal absence, migration, market movement |
| REL | Religious and inter-community relations | Cooperation, exchange, ritual participation, boundaries or tensions |
| GEN | Gender and everyday life | Gendered work, separation, care, communication and lived experience |
| RIT | Ritual and material culture | Festivals, offerings, tools, clothing, dwellings and productive technologies |
| POW | Status, authority and contestation | Land control, attached labour, hierarchy, claims to status or resistance |

#### Coding procedure

1. Give each passage a stable ID (P01–P20).
2. Record village, source, page and exact text.
3. Assign one or more thematic codes.
4. Record the actors, activity, exchange medium and historical context.
5. Add a short note explaining why each code was assigned.
6. Have a second team member independently review the coding of the most important passages. Discuss disagreements and record the final decision.
7. Count coded passages by theme and village, but also inspect the passages themselves.
8. Select a few strong passages for close reading in the final essay.

#### Outputs

- Qualitative coding spreadsheet/CSV.
- Theme-by-village matrix.
- A small bar chart or heatmap only if it clarifies the distribution.
- Selected, source-verified passages for the essay.

#### Historical interpretation

Use the coded passages to connect numerical patterns to lived practices. For example, a passage about a shepherd's seasonal absence may illuminate the social costs of transhumance; a description of khalian payments may show how customary exchange supported artisan work; a ritual passage may reveal how economic and religious relations intersected.

**Caution:** A count of coded passages measures the distribution of themes in this selected set of excerpts, not how common those experiences were in the historical population. Do not use the coding frequency as a prevalence estimate.

## 5B. How the four analyses fit together

The four analyses should not become four disconnected technical exercises. They should form one integrated evidentiary sequence:

| Analysis | Main question | Main output | Historical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comparative occupational/economic structure | How did the village economies differ? | Harmonised table and charts | Establishes local productive systems |
| Economic interdependence network | Who depended on whom, and through what exchanges? | Verified edge list and network | Explains relationships between groups |
| Historical classification crosswalk | How did sources classify work and communities over time? | Crosswalk and source-category matrix | Separates social change from classificatory change |
| Qualitative thematic coding | What do the passages reveal about lived experience and social meaning? | Coding matrix and selected excerpts | Adds context, voice and close-reading evidence |

Together, they support a broader argument:

**Ecological setting and productive resources → livelihood and occupational organisation → exchange and economic dependence → social hierarchy, negotiation and inter-community relations**, while source criticism asks how far the archive allows each link to be established.

This is an interpretive framework, not a claim that ecology mechanically determines social relations.

## 6. Build the historical comparison

The essay needs both a **spatial comparison** (three villages in 1961) and a **longitudinal comparison** (changing sources and categories between the late nineteenth century and 1961).

### 6.1 Spatial comparison: three village economies

| Dimension | Brahmaur | Chitrari | Maingal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ecological setting in Phase 2 | Alpine, approximately 7,000 ft | Terraced basin, approximately 5,800 ft | Forested slope, approximately 4,000 ft |

### 6.2 Longitudinal comparison: 1881–1961

Organise the chronology around what each source can reveal:

- **1881 Census:** Colonial enumeration and descriptions of Gaddi/Gujjar communities; use the cited dual-tenure evidence to discuss mobility and overlapping political jurisdictions, if verified.
- **Rose glossary (exact edition/date to be confirmed):** Ethnographic classification and the language used to rank or stereotype communities. Analyse the author's categories as evidence about colonial knowledge-making, not as neutral facts about the communities.
- **1931 Census:** Occupational and economic-dependency categories, including principal earners and working/non-working dependents as described in Phase 2. Explain what this classification makes visible and what it obscures.
- **1961 village monographs:** Detailed village-level evidence about households, livestock, occupations, exchange and cultural life. These provide depth but are later snapshots, not a continuous annual record.

**Critical rule:** The sources do not necessarily track the same individuals, households or categories over eighty years. Do not claim that a community's occupation changed from one form to another unless the sources actually establish that transition. Where direct continuity cannot be shown, describe a change in the way the region was documented, or compare structures and patterns with an explicit qualification.

## 8. Source criticism: questions to address throughout the essay

Do not confine source criticism to a short limitations paragraph at the end. Integrate it wherever a claim depends on a source.

### Colonial sources
- Who produced the census or glossary, for what administrative purpose, and using which categories?
- Do labels such as caste, tribe, occupation or dependency describe lived identities, administrative classifications, or both?
- Does Rose's language reproduce stereotypes or rank groups? Quote only what is necessary and analyse the wording.
- How might winter census timing affect the enumeration of mobile pastoralists?

### 1961 village surveys
- Who collected the information, and whose voices are directly represented?
- Are the surveys describing observed practice, customary rules, official categories or informants' accounts?
- Are women, landless workers, mobile groups and household-level disagreements adequately represented?
- Does a village monograph's detailed account justify a claim about the entire Chamba region? Usually not without further evidence.

### Computational evidence
- OCR errors can produce false absences, misspellings and incorrect counts.
- Keyword frequency measures textual occurrence, not population or importance.
- A zero count does not prove that a group was absent.
- Different monograph lengths and writing styles require normalization, but normalization does not remove every source bias.
- A network records documented exchanges, not every actual relationship.
- A graph edge needs a source passage; an algorithm cannot establish a relationship that the source does not document.

### Interpretation
For each major finding, follow this sequence:

**Observation → Source verification → Comparison → Interpretation → Alternative explanation → Limitation.**

This progression is already used in Phase 2 and should be retained in the final essay.

## 11. What not to spend time doing

Given the short time remaining, avoid work that does not materially strengthen the historical argument:

- Do not add a new NLP model, machine-learning classifier or complex network metric merely to make the project appear more computational.
- Do not generate more charts unless each one answers a clear historical question.
- Do not repeat the entire OCR pipeline in the final report. Summarise it and explain the choices relevant to the findings.
- Do not treat word frequency as a proxy for population, status or economic power.
- Do not claim that caste relations disappeared, that markets replaced custom, or that religious coexistence was universally harmonious unless the sources establish those claims.
- Do not generalise from three village monographs to all of Chamba or the Western Himalayas without qualification.
- Do not use the 1961 evidence as if it directly describes conditions in 1881.
- Do not leave citations, source verification or the integration of team sections until the final hours.

## 12. The standard the final essay should meet

A strong Phase 3 essay will make a reader understand:

1. **What changed or differed:** the occupational, ecological and exchange patterns visible in the sources.
2. **How the patterns were established:** the archival evidence, structured datasets, text analysis, comparisons and close reading.
3. **Why they mattered:** how production, labour, property, mobility and ecological constraints shaped social relations.
4. **How the interpretation is qualified:** what colonial classification, source genre, OCR, scale mismatch and missing evidence prevent the project from claiming.

The central achievement should be a historical explanation of how locally distinct economies connected people through unequal but mutually consequential forms of labour and exchange—not simply a catalogue of castes, occupations, statistics or computational techniques.
